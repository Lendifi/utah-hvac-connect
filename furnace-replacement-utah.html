Why replacement comes up so often in Utah

Utah’s climate is heating-heavy. Salt Lake City sees roughly 3.6 times as many heating degree days as cooling degree days in a typical year (NOAA 1991–2020 normals), and most homes in Salt Lake and Utah counties heat with utility gas: 78.8% and 80.1% of occupied homes, respectively (ACS 2024 1-year, table B25040). Furnaces here do a lot of work every winter.

A lot of the south Salt Lake Valley and north Utah County was built in the late 1990s and 2000s. Many of those original furnaces are now more than 15 years old, which is when replacement conversations with shops become common. Our local pages show the housing-age numbers ZIP by ZIP.

Timing matters too. Shops are busiest during the December–January cold peak. If your furnace still runs, planning a replacement in fall or spring gives you time to collect bids calmly.

Repair or replace: questions to bring to the shop

We don’t tell you whether to repair or replace. That’s a judgment a licensed shop makes on site. Manufacturers commonly describe a typical gas furnace lifespan of roughly 15 to 20 years with maintenance (for example, American Standard), but age alone isn’t the answer. Questions that help the conversation:

  • How old is the furnace, and what has been repaired in the last few years?
  • What failed this time, and is the part still readily available?
  • Is there any safety concern (for example, with the heat exchanger or venting)? Can you show me what you found?
  • What would the repair cost compared with replacement, in writing?
  • If I replace it, do you recommend furnace-only, or should the AC or a heat pump be part of the discussion? (See AC and heat pump replacement.)

If heat is already out, start with our no-heat page or pick “Today” on the form.

What drives the price (and why we don’t list one)

Our editorial policy is to publish only cost figures we can source, and your real price comes from the contractor who sees your home. What we can tell you is what usually moves a furnace bid up or down, so you can compare bids line by line:

  • Efficiency. Moving from an older 80% AFUE furnace to a 90%-plus condensing model usually means new PVC venting and a condensate drain. Ask whether that work is included.
  • Sizing. Ask how the shop sized the unit (a room-by-room load calculation, often called Manual J, versus matching the old nameplate).
  • Blower and staging. Single-stage, two-stage, and modulating furnaces differ in comfort and price.
  • Ductwork, gas line, and electrical changes needed to fit the new unit.
  • Permit, inspection, haul-away, and warranty registration. Ask for each to be listed.
  • Rebate paperwork. Ask who files it and which model number qualifies.

A useful habit: get at least two written, itemized bids for the same scope, each with the model numbers and the contractor’s license number on it.

Permits and inspection

Furnace replacement involves gas piping, venting, and combustion air, so it normally needs a city mechanical permit and an inspection. For example, Sandy City lists an “A/C Furnace replacement” permit covering “the replacement or addition of any HVAC system” (Sandy residential mechanical permits), and West Jordan’s published permit reports list furnace and air-conditioner permits (West Jordan permit report). Ask your contractor whether the permit is in the bid and who schedules the inspection. Our local pages link to each city’s building office.

How to check a Utah HVAC license (about 2 minutes)

  1. Open the state lookup. Go to the Utah Division of Professional Licensing (DOPL) Licensee Lookup & Verification page. It’s free and public.
  2. Search by name or by license number. Under “Name and Profession,” type the company name (choose “containing” if the exact legal name differs from the truck sign) and tick Contractor to narrow results. If the shop printed a license number on its estimate or website, use “License Number” instead: the core number plus the four-digit identifier.
  3. Finish the “I’m not a robot” check and open the matching record.
  4. Read three things: the status (you want Active; ask questions about anything else, including “Active on Probation”), the classification (an HVAC classification such as H100, or the older S350), and the business name (it should match the company on your estimate).
  5. Ask for the license number in writing on the estimate, and keep a screenshot of the lookup with the date.

Why S350 still counts for now: under Utah’s administrative rules, a contractor holding an active S350 HVAC license in good standing on January 1, 2026 is treated as holding an H100 for the licensing period that ends November 30, 2027 (Utah Admin. Code R156-55a-301g(4)). Utah HVAC Connect only works with Utah-licensed contractors; see licenses and contractor partners.

FAQ

Do you sell furnaces or install them?

No. Utah HVAC Connect is a matchmaker. We connect homeowners with licensed Utah HVAC contractors. We don’t sell equipment, perform installs, diagnose systems, or provide quotes.

Is there a federal tax credit for a new furnace in 2026?

No. The IRS says the Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit (section 25C) isn’t allowed for property placed in service after December 31, 2025. Utility rebates, such as Enbridge Gas ThermWise for high-efficiency gas furnaces, are separate and were still listed in 2026.

What rebates are available for a new furnace in Utah?

Enbridge Gas ThermWise listed $300 for a 95%–97.4% AFUE gas furnace and $350 for 97.5% AFUE or higher with an ECM blower on October 7, 2026, with the request due within six months of installation. Dual-fuel systems can also qualify for ThermWise and, for Rocky Mountain Power customers, Wattsmart rebates. Confirm current amounts with each program.

Do I need a permit to replace a furnace in Utah?

Usually, yes. Furnace replacement involves gas piping, venting, and combustion air. Sandy City, for example, lists an “A/C Furnace replacement” mechanical permit for the replacement of any HVAC system, and West Jordan’s permit reports list furnace and air-conditioner permits. Ask your contractor whether the permit and inspection are included in the bid.

How do I check if an HVAC contractor is licensed in Utah?

Search the company on DOPL’s free Licensee Lookup & Verification site. Look for an Active status, an HVAC classification such as H100 (or the older S350), and a business name that matches your estimate.

How long does a furnace last?

Manufacturers commonly describe a typical gas furnace lifespan of roughly 15 to 20 years with maintenance, but age alone doesn’t decide replacement. A licensed shop looks at condition, safety, repair history, and parts availability.
